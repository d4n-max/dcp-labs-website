Preparing for a QA interview in seven days is challenging, but it is possible to make meaningful progress with a focused plan. The goal is not to memorize every testing term. It is to build a usable foundation, practise explaining your thinking, and notice the topics that still need review.

A short timeline works best when each day has one job. Keep sessions realistic—about 30 to 60 minutes—and use active practice instead of reading notes from beginning to end. This seven-day QA interview preparation plan is designed for beginners and for candidates who need to refresh familiar material.

## Day 1: Map the role and refresh testing foundations

Start by reading the job description carefully. Highlight the tools, testing types, product area, and collaboration skills it mentions. This tells you where to spend limited study time. A role focused on web applications may call for more browser, API, and regression examples than a role focused on mobile testing.

Then review the foundations you should be able to explain in plain language:

- The purpose of quality assurance and the difference between finding defects and preventing them.
- Functional versus non-functional testing.
- Test cases, test scenarios, test suites, and acceptance criteria.
- Verification versus validation.
- The stages of a typical software development life cycle.

Do not aim for polished textbook definitions. Write a one- or two-sentence explanation for each term and add a simple example from a login page, checkout flow, or another familiar product.

## Day 2: Practise writing test cases

Interviewers often want to see how you turn a requirement into coverage. Choose a small feature, such as a password reset form, and write test cases that cover the normal path and meaningful variations.

For each case, record the preconditions, steps, test data, and expected result. Include at least one case for each of these areas:

- A valid input and successful outcome.
- Empty, missing, or incorrectly formatted input.
- Minimum and maximum boundaries.
- Repeated actions, refreshes, and navigation back and forward.
- Different user permissions or device sizes when relevant.

Practise explaining why you selected each case. That reasoning matters more than producing a long list. A concise set of high-value tests shows prioritisation.

## Day 3: Learn to describe useful bug reports

Take two of yesterday’s failed scenarios and turn them into bug reports. A good report helps another person reproduce the problem without guessing.

Include a short, specific title; the environment; preconditions; steps to reproduce; expected result; actual result; and supporting evidence when available. Be careful with severity and priority: a defect can be severe but not urgent in a particular release, or urgent without threatening the whole system.

Practise saying what you would do when a developer cannot reproduce the issue. You could confirm the build and test data, capture logs or video, try a second environment, and ask focused questions. The aim is collaborative investigation, not blame.

## Day 4: Review common QA scenarios

Use scenario questions to practise your decision-making. For example, explain how you would test a login form, what you would check after a production fix, or how you would approach an unfamiliar feature with incomplete requirements.

A reliable answer structure is:

1. Clarify the goal, users, risks, and constraints.
2. Identify the main user journey and likely failure points.
3. Choose test data and environments.
4. Run high-risk checks first, then broaden coverage.
5. Record evidence, report defects, and retest fixes.

This structure keeps an answer organised even when the question is new. Say what you would prioritise and why rather than claiming you would test everything immediately.

## Day 5: Rehearse communication and teamwork answers

QA work includes communication, so prepare examples about handling disagreement, working under a deadline, and raising a risk early. Use a simple situation, task, action, and result structure. Keep the focus on what you did and learned.

Avoid presenting yourself as someone who blocks releases automatically. A stronger answer explains how you communicate risk, document evidence, and help the team make an informed decision. If you have no professional example, use a course project or personal practice project and label it honestly.

## Day 6: Run a timed mock interview

Set a timer for 30 minutes and answer a mix of definition, test-design, bug-report, and behavioural questions. Speak your answers aloud. Notice whether you start with the conclusion, use concrete examples, and leave room for clarification.

Afterward, score each answer from one to five for accuracy, structure, and clarity. Turn weak answers into short question-and-answer flashcards. Reviewing the cards later is more effective than rereading the entire study session because it requires you to retrieve the answer.

## Day 7: Consolidate and protect your energy

Use the final day for targeted review, not an all-day cram session. Revisit the terms, scenarios, and questions you scored lowest. Prepare a short list of questions to ask the interviewer about the team’s testing process, release workflow, tools, and expectations for the role.

Before the interview, check your setup, confirm the time zone and meeting link, and keep your notes nearby. Sleep and a calm start are part of preparation. You want enough energy to listen carefully and think through unfamiliar questions.
