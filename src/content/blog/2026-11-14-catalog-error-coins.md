Error coins are exciting because a small difference can turn an ordinary-looking piece into an important research question. They are also easy to miscatalog. A scratch, stain, dent, or piece of circulation wear can look unusual without being a mint-made error.

A good catalog does not need to prove a coin is valuable or authentic. It needs to preserve what you observed, show what still needs checking, and make the coin easy to find again. That record helps you compare references, ask a specialist a focused question, and avoid relying on memory months later.

## Start with a neutral description

Before assigning an error type, record the coin as if you were describing it to someone who cannot see it. Include:

- Country and denomination, if known
- Date and mint mark
- Metal or apparent composition
- Obverse and reverse design
- Edge type, such as plain, reeded, lettered, or decorated
- Weight and diameter, if you have measured them
- Where and when you found or acquired it

This baseline matters because an unusual feature can be easier to understand when the rest of the coin is documented. It also protects you from building the entire record around an early guess.

If a detail is uncertain, say so. “Possibly 1980s United States quarter” is more useful than silently choosing a date that makes a theory fit. You can update a field later while keeping the original observation in your notes.

## Photograph both sides and the edge

Photos are the most useful evidence in an error-coin record. Take a straight-on image of the full obverse, the full reverse, and the edge. Add close-ups of the unusual area from more than one angle.

Try to keep the setup consistent:

1. Use soft, even light so raised details do not disappear in glare.
2. Keep the camera parallel to the coin to reduce distortion.
3. Include a scale or record the camera distance when size matters.
4. Photograph the same coin orientation each time.
5. Keep the original, uncropped files as well as any edited versions.

A close-up can show a doubled design element, missing plating, or an unusual die mark. The full-coin images provide context. Together they make it easier to distinguish a localized feature from damage that affects a larger area.

## Record the suspected error without overstating it

Use a field such as “suspected error” or “working classification.” Write what you think you see and why, but keep the language provisional until it has been confirmed.

Examples include:

- Suspected doubled die on the date
- Possible off-center strike
- Possible clipped planchet
- Suspected broadstrike
- Possible die break near the rim
- Unusual color or surface that may be post-mint damage

Add the location and direction of the feature. “Raised line near the reverse rim at roughly 4 o’clock” is much more useful than “weird mark on back.” Note whether the feature appears raised, incuse, repeated, or flat. That distinction can guide the next research step, while still leaving room to revise the classification.

## Separate mint-made features from damage

Cataloging is a record-keeping task, not a promise that every unusual coin is an error. Common post-mint causes include scratches, cleaning, dents, environmental discoloration, machine doubling, and damage from a coin-counting machine.

Create a simple “evidence” note with observations on both sides. Does the feature interrupt the design in a way that would be difficult to create after striking? Does it continue across a raised design element? Is metal pushed up around a mark, or has metal been removed? Does the edge show a related sign?

These questions do not replace an expert opinion. They simply make your record more disciplined and reduce the chance of treating an attractive but ordinary flaw as a confirmed variety.

## Add a confirmation status

A status field keeps uncertainty visible. You might use:

- Unreviewed: unusual feature recorded, no comparison completed
- Researching: checking references and comparable examples
- Likely damage: evidence points to handling or environmental wear
- Possible mint error: feature remains consistent with a mint-made issue
- Confirmed by reference: matches a documented variety or error
- Expert review requested: waiting for an outside assessment

Include the date of each status change and a short reason. For example, “Changed to likely damage after comparing the line with examples of contact marks.” This history prevents old assumptions from being mistaken for current conclusions.

## Track provenance and storage

Record how the coin entered your collection: purchase, inherited group, roll search, trade, or found in circulation. Keep seller notes, receipts, lot numbers, and any previous descriptions when available. Provenance does not prove an error, but it gives useful context and helps you locate supporting information.

Also note the physical storage location. A tray number, album page, flip identifier, or box code is enough. If the coin moves, update the location at the same time you update the catalog. A detailed record is less useful if you cannot reconnect it to the physical coin.

## Keep research notes connected to the coin

Save the sources you consulted, not just your conclusion. A note can include a reference title, page, URL, publication date, or the search terms that led you there. Add links to comparable photos when the source permits it, and explain what matched or did not match.

This approach makes future review faster. It also helps you ask a specialist a precise question: “Does the raised doubling on the last digit continue through the design, or is this machine doubling?” is easier to answer than “Is this rare?”

## Use a digital inventory for repeatable records

Paper notes can work for a small group, but suspected errors often need several photos, updates, and cross-references. A digital inventory lets you search by date, mint mark, suspected error type, confirmation status, or storage location.

The goal is not to turn every unusual coin into a valuation claim. It is to create a reliable trail from observation to research to confirmation. That trail remains useful whether the coin turns out to be a documented error, ordinary damage, or simply an interesting piece of your collection.

## A practical error-coin catalog checklist

Before you put the coin away, check that the record includes:

- Full obverse, reverse, and edge photos
- Date, mint mark, denomination, and country
- Weight, diameter, and composition notes when available
- A neutral description of the unusual feature
- A provisional error classification
- Evidence for and against a mint-made explanation
- Confirmation status and review date
- Acquisition details and storage location
- References or questions for the next research step

Cataloging this way keeps curiosity separate from certainty. You preserve the evidence, make your next step clear, and build a collection that is easier to understand over time.
