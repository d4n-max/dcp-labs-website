When a pet emergency happens, the hardest part is often finding ordinary information quickly. A sitter may know your dog’s name but not the clinic’s phone number. A family member may know where the food is but not which medication was last given. A well-made pet emergency information sheet puts the essentials in one place before anyone has to search.

This sheet is an organization tool, not veterinary advice. It should help a caregiver contact the right people, follow the instructions you have already received, and bring useful records to a veterinary professional.

## Start with the details that identify your pet

Put the identity section at the top so there is no ambiguity if several pets are involved. Include:

- Pet name, preferred name, species, breed or coat description, and sex
- Current photo, especially if your pet is shy, recently groomed, or similar to another household pet
- Date of birth or best estimate, plus microchip number and registration details if applicable
- Your name, phone number, address, and a second trusted contact
- Any handling notes, such as whether your pet needs a carrier, leash, muzzle, or extra space

Keep the wording factual and specific. “Hides when frightened” is more useful than “can be difficult.” Note what helps your pet settle and what a caregiver should avoid.

## List the contacts in the order someone should use them

Emergency information is easier to follow when the contact sequence is obvious. Make the phone numbers large enough to read at a glance and label each one clearly:

1. Your primary contact and backup contact
2. Regular veterinary clinic, including address and opening hours
3. Preferred emergency clinic or after-hours hospital
4. Pet insurance provider, if you have a policy
5. Trusted sitter, neighbor, family member, or transportation contact

Confirm the clinic details periodically. A printed sheet can become outdated when a practice changes its hours or moves location, so add a “last checked” date at the bottom. If your area has more than one emergency facility, explain which one you prefer and why, without assuming it will always be open.

## Record medications and care instructions carefully

Medication details should come from the label, prescription, or veterinary instructions you already have. For each current medication or preventive, record:

- Product or medication name and the form it takes
- The instruction as provided by the clinic or label
- When it was last given, if known, and where the medication is stored
- The prescribing clinic and the date of the most recent instruction
- Any administration notes, such as whether it is mixed with food

Avoid writing a new dose from memory or copying an old instruction that may no longer apply. If a caregiver is unsure what to give, when to give it, or whether a dose was missed, the sheet should direct them to call the clinic rather than improvise.

For recurring care, include a simple status line such as “next confirmed appointment: 12 November” or “refill to discuss with clinic.” This gives a caregiver context without turning the sheet into a replacement for professional guidance.

## Add allergies, sensitivities, and important history

Make a short, prominent section for information a clinic or caregiver should see early. Depending on your pet’s records, this may include:

- Known allergies or past reactions
- Food sensitivities that have been documented
- Ongoing conditions or mobility limitations, described in the same plain language used by your veterinarian
- Recent procedures, hospital visits, or follow-up instructions
- Vaccination record location and the date it was last updated

Do not guess at a diagnosis. If a past reaction is uncertain, label it as “owner-reported” and include the date or source. The goal is to give a professional a useful starting point and point them to the original record.

## Explain the normal routine

In a stressful moment, a caregiver benefits from knowing what is normal for your pet. Add a small routine section covering:

- Usual feeding times and the food currently used
- Water, litter, toilet, walk, or outdoor habits
- Typical energy, sleep, and behavior
- Comfort items, hiding places, and safe handling preferences
- Where leads, carriers, food, cleaning supplies, and documents are stored

Keep this section practical rather than exhaustive. A short baseline can help someone notice a change and describe it accurately when speaking with a veterinary professional.

## Attach the records that support the sheet

The summary is most useful when it points to the source documents. Keep copies or clear photos of:

- Vaccination and microchip records
- Current prescriptions and medication labels
- Recent invoices, discharge notes, or lab results you were given
- Insurance policy and claim details
- Adoption, registration, or travel documents when relevant

Use consistent filenames and dates, such as `2026-10-27-vaccine-record.pdf`. If the original document is on paper, photograph all pages and check that names and dates are readable. Keep a backup in a secure location, and do not include sensitive information that a casual sitter does not need.

## Make a version for sitters and a fuller version for you

One document does not have to serve every audience. A sitter may need contacts, routine, access instructions, and approved care tasks. You may also keep a fuller owner record with historical notes, older documents, and questions for the next appointment.

Mark the sitter version with an update date and tell the caregiver where the latest copy lives. When responsibilities are shared, ask the person receiving the handoff to repeat back the key steps. That simple check can reveal a missing phone number or an unclear instruction before it matters.

## Keep the sheet current without creating more work

Review the sheet after an appointment, medication change, move, new diagnosis, or change in caregiver. A five-minute monthly check is enough for most households:

1. Verify contact numbers and clinic hours.
2. Remove discontinued instructions from the active section while preserving original records.
3. Update the current food, medication, and routine details.
4. Confirm that linked documents still open and are readable.
5. Share the current version with the people who may need it.

[CareTail](/caretail) can fit this workflow by keeping pet profiles, reminders, diary notes, and documents together. That gives you a calmer place to maintain the information behind an emergency sheet, while veterinary professionals remain the source for medical decisions.

An emergency information sheet works best when it is clear, current, and easy to access. Build the first version on a quiet day, keep the wording simple, and update it whenever your pet’s care or your household changes.
