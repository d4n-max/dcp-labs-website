Good coin photos do more than make a collection look polished. They preserve details you may want to inspect later, help you compare similar pieces, and give your identification notes useful evidence. A blurry or reflective image can hide the date, mint mark, edge, or surface feature that makes one coin different from another.

You do not need expensive camera equipment to photograph coins well. A phone, a steady setup, soft light, and a repeatable process are enough for most collection records. The aim is not to create an auction catalog image or prove a coin's value from a photograph. It is to make the visible details easy to review and to keep a reliable visual record beside your written notes.

## Start with a clean, safe setup

Choose a stable surface away from clutter, direct sunlight, and strong overhead reflections. A plain matte background makes the coin easier to see and keeps the camera's exposure from being distracted by bright patterns. Dark gray, white, or a muted blue can all work; consistency matters more than the exact color.

Handle the coin by its edges whenever possible. Oils from fingers can leave marks, and rubbing a surface before photographing it can remove useful evidence of its condition. Do not polish, wash, or scrape a coin to make the picture look better. A record should show the coin as you found it.

Place the phone or camera on a small stand, tripod, or stack of stable books. If you must hold it, brace your elbows and use a timer so pressing the shutter does not introduce motion blur. Turn off portrait blur and filters. Identification photos should be accurate, not stylized.

## Use soft, even lighting

The most common coin photography problem is glare. A bare desk lamp aimed straight at a coin creates a bright hotspot and can wash out lettering. Instead, use a large, soft light source from the side. Daylight through a window with a thin curtain can work, as can a lamp bounced off a white wall or sheet of paper.

Try moving the light and camera a few degrees at a time. A small change in angle can reveal raised lettering, a mint mark, or a doubled detail without creating a hard reflection. If the coin is highly reflective, place a sheet of white paper near the camera so the coin reflects a bright, even surface rather than the dark phone body.

Avoid mixing very different light sources. A warm lamp and cool daylight can make the metal color look inconsistent. Color accuracy is helpful when you are comparing copper, silver-colored alloys, or toning, so use one light source for the main record whenever possible.

## Fill the frame without cutting off the edge

Position the coin so the complete rim is visible, with a little space around it. A close crop can hide edge lettering, clipped planchets, irregular rims, or the relationship between the coin and its holder. Keep the camera parallel to the coin rather than shooting from a steep angle; this reduces distortion and keeps circular coins looking circular.

Tap the date or central design on your phone screen to set focus. Check the resulting image at full size before moving on. A photo that looks sharp in a thumbnail may be soft around the mint mark or small lettering. If your camera supports focus lock, use it after the coin is centered.

Do not rely on digital zoom. Move the camera closer while keeping the entire coin in frame, or use the phone's optical macro mode if it produces a genuinely sharper result. Leave enough resolution to crop a detail later without making the original record too small.

## Photograph both faces and the edge

For a useful identification record, photograph at least three views:

- the obverse, showing the full front and all surrounding lettering;
- the reverse, showing the full back, denomination, date context, and symbols;
- the edge, especially when it is reeded, lettered, plain, or visibly unusual.

Keep the orientation consistent. For example, rotate every coin so the top of the design is in the same direction, then use the same order for front and back images. This makes later comparison faster and reduces the chance of attaching a reverse photo to the wrong entry.

An edge image does not need to be elaborate. Stand the coin carefully on its edge against a support, or hold it by the rim while keeping fingers outside the area of interest. If the coin is fragile or valuable, photograph it in its holder and note that fact rather than taking unnecessary handling risks.

## Add a scale and a reference when useful

A ruler or coin-sized measurement reference can help when the denomination is unknown or when you are comparing similarly designed pieces. Place the reference beside the coin, not on top of it, and keep it in the same plane so the size relationship is honest. A small label with an inventory number can also be useful, provided it does not cover any part of the coin.

Scale does not replace written measurements. Record diameter and, when relevant, weight in the catalog as separate fields. A photograph can show a rough relationship, but it cannot reliably establish exact specifications by itself.

## Capture detail shots only after the overview

Start with the full-coin views before taking close-ups. Once those are saved, make detail images for the date, mint mark, possible error, unusual surface mark, or edge feature. Use the same lighting and focus process for each close-up, and label what the image is meant to show.

Close-ups are especially useful when you are uncertain. A note such as “possible doubling near the date” is more useful when it points to a specific image. At the same time, avoid treating a dramatic-looking close-up as proof of a rare variety. Record what is visible, keep the wording tentative, and compare it with reliable references before changing the identification.

## Keep the photo and catalog record together

File names should be simple and consistent. An inventory number, date, and view label are enough, such as `CR-0042-obverse`, `CR-0042-reverse`, and `CR-0042-edge`. Avoid names based only on a guess like `rare-coin-final.jpg`; guesses can change as research improves.

Alongside the photos, record the country, denomination, year, mint mark, measurements, storage location, source, and any uncertainty. Add the date the photos were taken if the coin's condition or your notes may change over time. Keep the original images, not only edited crops, so you can revisit the evidence.

It is most useful when it supports the habit you already have: photograph the coin, save what you know, and leave a clear place for questions that still need research.

## A quick coin photography checklist

Before you finish an entry, check that:

- the background is plain and the coin is shown as found;
- lighting is soft enough that lettering is not hidden by glare;
- the entire obverse and reverse are in focus;
- the edge is recorded when it carries useful information;
- a scale or measurement is included when identification is uncertain;
- close-ups are labeled as details, not treated as separate coins;
- the original photos use the same inventory number as the written record.

Clear images will not identify every coin automatically, and they cannot establish authenticity or a guaranteed value. They do give you a much stronger starting point for careful research. With a steady setup and consistent file-and-note habits, photographing coins becomes a small routine that protects the story of your collection.
